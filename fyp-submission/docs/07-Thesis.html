Abstract

Online marketplace buyers routinely face fraud from fake seller profiles, stolen product photos, throwaway phone numbers and email addresses, multiple accounts created from a single device, and listings that are "too good to be true," with no reliable way to judge a seller before paying. TrustLens addresses this by combining seven independent verification signals — identity (CNIC optical character recognition plus selfie-to-document face matching), phone, email, device, network, account behaviour, and AI-assisted listing analysis — into a single, explainable Trust Score between 0 and 100, with a risk band and the specific reasons behind it. The central design commitment is that the score is computed by a deterministic, versioned rule engine, never directly by an AI model: AI components (OCR, face matching, a large-language-model listing analyser, and a custom-trained scam-listing classifier) supply typed evidence with a confidence value, which the rule engine converts into points. This makes every score reproducible and appealable by construction. The system was built as a modular monolith — a thin, synchronous FastAPI backend, an asynchronous ARQ worker for AI-heavy processing, a Next.js frontend, and a framework-free domain package enforcing clean-architecture boundaries — and runs entirely offline at zero recurring cost, a deliberate constraint for a two-student, self-funded FYP. As of this report, the core seller journey (registration through identity/phone/email verification, score computation, and appeal resolution) works end to end and is covered by 330 passing automated tests; the administrative panel and public deployment remain partially complete, and no accuracy figures exist yet for OCR or face matching against real identity documents, since collecting a consented real-image test set was assigned as ongoing student work rather than an engineering task. A cross-corpus evaluation of the custom scam-listing classifier revealed a significant, honestly-reported weakness — strong performance (0.906 F1) on its own training-domain data but poor performance (catching only 1 of 7 real Pakistani marketplace scam listings in a manual check) on the product's actual target text — which is why the classifier is not yet enabled as a live scoring input. This report documents the system's requirements, design, implementation, and evaluation in full, including these limitations, on the view that an honestly reported partial result defends better at viva than an inflated claim.

Keywords: trust score, seller verification, explainable AI, fraud detection, identity verification, marketplace security, clean architecture.

Chapter 1 — Introduction

1.1 Background

Online marketplaces (OLX-style classifieds, rental and property portals, second-hand vehicle sites, and freelance platforms) connect strangers for a transaction with very little institutional trust infrastructure between them. Unlike a licensed retailer, an individual seller on such a platform carries almost no verifiable history a buyer can inspect before paying.

1.2 Problem statement

The problem decomposes into five concrete, recurring fraud sub-problems, each named directly in the approved project proposal [12]:

  1. Fake seller profiles — a seller's claimed identity cannot be verified by a buyer.
  2. Stolen product photos — images lifted from other listings or the wider web misrepresent what is actually for sale.
  3. Throwaway phone numbers and email addresses — contact channels that stop responding after payment, with no cost to abandon.
  4. Multiple accounts from one device — a banned or flagged seller re-enters under a new identity from the same hardware.
  5. Unrealistic, "too good to be true" listings — pricing and language patterns typical of advance-fee and non-delivery scams.

No existing mechanism combines checks against all five into one buyer-facing signal that explains itself.

1.3 Motivation

The two most common existing responses — a simple phone-OTP gate (as many marketplaces already implement) or an unstructured star-rating/review system — each address only a narrow slice of the problem and neither is explainable. A phone OTP proves control of a number, not identity, and not listing honesty. A star rating requires a completed transaction history to exist before it says anything useful, which is exactly the information a first-time buyer lacks. This motivated a design that (a) checks several independent signals rather than one, and (b) always shows its reasoning, rather than asking a buyer to trust an opaque score.

1.5 Scope

In scope: the eight features listed in the approved proposal (identity, phone, email, and device checks; AI listing analysis; the scoring engine; an admin/appeal panel; a public API) — see the SRS (03-SRS.md) §1.2 for the full in/out-of-scope tables.

Explicitly out of scope, per the proposal's own stated future scope: reverse-image stolen-photo detection, chat-based scam detection, direct marketplace integrations, seller reputation history over time, and a mobile application. Also out of scope, and stated here rather than discovered later: any claim of government-grade identity verification (no public NADRA API exists at FYP access level), and validated real-world OCR/face-match accuracy figures (no consented real test-image set exists yet).

1.6 Limitations, stated up front

Stating limitations before the results chapter, rather than only at the end, is a deliberate choice so a reader calibrates expectations correctly from the start rather than being surprised later:

  • No real CNIC/selfie test images have been benchmarked yet — accuracy claims for OCR and face matching are architectural/contractual (the engines are correctly wired and pass synthetic-fixture tests), not empirically validated against real documents.
  • The custom-trained scam-listing classifier performs poorly on the product's actual target text (see §7 for the measured numbers) and is deliberately not yet a live scoring input.
  • The administrative panel is functionally incomplete — only the identity/appeal review queue and scoring-model view/simulate/activate flows work end-to-end.
  • Multi-factor authentication for staff accounts is designed but not implemented.
  • No public deployment exists at the time of writing; the system runs correctly locally via Docker.

Chapter 2 — Literature Review

2.1 Trust and reputation systems in e-commerce

The general problem of establishing trust between strangers transacting online is well studied. Resnick and Zeckhauser's foundational work on eBay's feedback mechanism, and subsequent surveys and mechanism-design analyses of trust and reputation systems in e-commerce [1][2][3][11], establish that reputation systems broadly fall into two families: outcome-based systems (star ratings, feedback scores, accumulated after a transaction completes) and attribute-based systems (verified credentials, checked before a transaction). A comprehensive survey of e-commerce trust and reputation mechanisms [2] compares systems on utility, usability, performance, and effectiveness, and notes that outcome-based systems structurally cannot help a first-time buyer evaluating a seller with no transaction history — exactly the gap TrustLens targets by being attribute-based (identity, contact, device, and listing checks that exist before any transaction occurs) rather than outcome-based.

2.2 Automated fraudulent-text detection

The AI listing-analysis component (F5) draws directly on research into detecting fraudulent job postings, a closely related text-classification problem: distinguishing genuine adverts from scam/fraudulent ones based on language patterns (urgency, unrealistic offers, requests to move off-platform). Published work on the Employment Scam Aegean Dataset (EMSCAD) [4][5] establishes that classical and ensemble machine-learning classifiers (Random Forest, and deep architectures combining CNN/LSTM layers over text embeddings) can achieve high accuracy — commonly cited in the 90%+ range — on their own training-domain data. TrustLens's own scam-listing classifier is trained partly on EMSCAD and a second proxy fraud corpus (DIFrauD), which is precisely why its strong own-corpus F1 score (0.906, Chapter 6) closely matches the accuracy ranges reported in this literature — and precisely why the same literature's implicit assumption (that a classifier's own-corpus performance predicts its real-world performance) was worth testing rather than trusting, because the cross-corpus results in Chapter 6 show that assumption failing badly for this specific domain transfer (English job adverts → Pakistani marketplace listings).

2.3 Identity document verification and forgery detection

Automated identity-document verification (the OCR half of Feature F1) is an active research and commercial area. Recent work on document-forgery detection combines OCR-based preprocessing with convolutional and transformer architectures to detect manipulated identity documents [6][7][8], and a 2026 systematic survey of identity-document attack and detection methods [9] documents that production KYC (Know Your Customer) systems increasingly face AI-generated and synthetically forged documents as an emerging threat class. TrustLens's design deliberately does not attempt forgery detection in this sense — it performs OCR field extraction and plausibility checks (expiry date, format, quality gates) rather than adversarial forgery detection, which is a materially harder research problem placed outside this FYP's scope, and this report says so rather than implying a stronger guarantee.

2.4 Face-recognition accuracy and demographic fairness

The face-match component's design (the deliberate 0.55–0.70 "manual review" band, rather than a single hard threshold) is directly informed by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology's Face Recognition Vendor Test, Part 3: Demographic Effects [10], which found that the majority of tested face-recognition algorithms exhibit measurable false-positive and false-negative rate disparities across demographic groups, with the gap between the best- and worst-performing algorithms spanning false-negative rates from under 1% to as high as 99% depending on the demographic slice and algorithm tested. This is the specific research basis for TrustLens's design decision (SDD §8.3) to route borderline face-match results to a human reviewer rather than an automatic rejection — a system-level mitigation for a known, externally-validated technical limitation, rather than an assumption that the underlying face-matching library is unbiased.

Chapter 4 — System Design

Condensed from the full Software Design Document, 04-SDD.md, which contains the complete diagram set (20+ Mermaid diagrams), full data dictionary, and design-decision rationale table. That document remains authoritative for implementation detail.

4.1 Architecture overview

TrustLens is a modular monolith with an out-of-process worker, not a microservices system — a deliberate choice for a two-person team and one bounded context (SDD §2.4). Four deployable units share two codebases:

  • apps/web (Next.js) — all human-facing UI: public score lookup, seller portal, admin panel.
  • apps/api (FastAPI) — thin, synchronous HTTP surface; never runs AI-heavy work in the request path.
  • apps/worker (ARQ over Redis) — OCR, face matching, LLM calls, classifier inference, and score computation, all asynchronous.
  • apps/ml — an offline training pipeline for the scam-listing classifier, versioned separately from the serving path.

Both apps/api and apps/worker depend on a framework-free domain package (packages/py/trustlens-core) that defines entities, use cases, and ports (Protocol interfaces) with zero imports of FastAPI, SQLAlchemy, or any AI vendor SDK — enforced in CI, not just by convention.

4.2 Data design

The database holds 40 PostgreSQL tables. The two that matter most for the product's central claim are trust_scores (the score itself, immutable and append-only, so history is never lost) and trust_score_signals (one row per contributing signal per score, carrying its status, points, and a human-readable reason) — together, these are what make a score explainable without recomputation. A partial unique index guarantees a seller can never have more than one current score, enforced by the database itself rather than application discipline.

4.3 The Trust Score algorithm

Fourteen signals across seven categories sum to 100 possible points. Three properties distinguish this from a naive weighted average:

  1. Signals that could not be evaluated are excluded from both the numerator and denominator, never scored as a failure — protecting a seller from being penalised for a third-party outage that was not their fault.
  2. Critical-signal failures apply a hard cap on the final score regardless of how well every other signal scored — closing the specific exploit where a seller passes every easy check while failing the one hard-to-fake check (identity face match) and would otherwise still land in the "low risk" band.
  3. Insufficient total evidence yields a fourth state, UNKNOWN, distinct from and never confusable with HIGH risk — a brand-new honest seller is not the same thing as a proven scammer, and the design treats them differently.

4.4 Security design highlights

Password hashing (argon2id), envelope-encrypted CNIC/DOB fields, refresh-token rotation with reuse detection, mandatory object-level ownership checks (preventing IDOR), and a deny-by-default routing assertion that fails the application's own startup if any non-public route lacks an authorization dependency. The most domain-specific control addresses prompt injection: because a seller's own listing text is passed to an LLM, that text is treated as untrusted data whose output is schema-validated and range-checked, never as instructions the model could follow — the worst a successful injection can achieve is a wrong label on one 10-point signal, visible and appealable.

4.5 Key design decisions

The single design decision most worth defending at viva is ADR-0002: a deterministic rule engine computes the score; AI supplies evidence, never the verdict. The alternative — letting an LLM or classifier output the score directly — was rejected because it is neither reproducible (the same inputs could yield different scores on a re-ask) nor appealable (a reviewer cannot audit "the model said so"). The full design-decisions table with every alternative considered is SDD §12.

Because the scoring policy is pure, dependency-free code, it carries the most thoroughly executed test coverage in the project: all-PASS → 100, all-FAIL → 0, an unavailable signal excluded from the denominator (confirmed: a seller with one 10-point signal unavailable still scores 100, not 90, when everything else passes), a hard cap correctly clamping a would-be-85 score to 25 when identity face match fails, and exact risk-band boundary behaviour at 39/40 and 69/70.

7.4 Discussion — what the design choices turned out to matter for in practice

The decision to make hard caps and the UNAVAILABLE-signal exclusion explicit, rather than relying on a plain weighted average, was justified on paper in Chapter 4 — but its practical value only became fully visible while building the classifier evaluation in Chapter 6: had the classifier's weak cross-domain accuracy been wired directly into a weighted average, its false negatives would have quietly inflated real scammers' scores with no visible sign anything was wrong. Because the design already separates "evidence" from "score-setting," the fix was simply not enabling that evidence source yet — no scoring-engine change was needed, which is exactly the kind of resilience the architecture was intended to provide, discovered as a genuine (if slightly uncomfortable) validation of an earlier design decision rather than planned in advance.

Comparison with the related work in Chapter 2: unlike the commercial KYC vendors and the outcome-based reputation systems surveyed there, TrustLens's contribution is specifically the combination of attribute-based checks into one explainable, appealable number — not any single check in isolation, each of which individually resembles prior work.

7.5 Ethical and safety discussion

The system processes national identity documents and biometric face data for a real population. Design responses already in place: explicit versioned consent before any biometric processing, 90-day retention limits on raw documents, encryption of extracted sensitive fields, and — directly informed by the NIST demographic-fairness findings in Chapter 2 — a deliberate human-review band for borderline face-match results rather than a hard automatic threshold. The system explicitly does not claim to perform government identity verification, which would both overstate its actual capability and create a false sense of certainty for buyers.

Chapter 8 — Conclusion and Future Work

8.1 Conclusion

TrustLens demonstrates that combining several independent, individually-weak verification signals into one deterministically-scored, fully explainable Trust Score is both technically achievable within a two-student FYP's resources and materially more useful to a buyer than any single existing mechanism (a phone gate, a star rating) reviewed in Chapter 2. The core engineering — a clean-architecture domain package, an asynchronous AI pipeline kept out of the request path, and a scoring engine that treats AI output as evidence rather than verdict — is built, tested, and working. The project's honesty about what remains incomplete (admin panel, MFA, real-image accuracy validation, and the classifier's real weakness) is presented as a strength of this report, not a weakness of the project: an FYP that states its real limitations plainly is more defensible at viva than one that implies a completeness it has not achieved.

8.2 Contributions

  1. A deterministic, versioned scoring engine that converts probabilistic AI evidence into an explainable, appealable score — with unavailable evidence excluded rather than penalised.
  2. A working, end-to-end seller verification and appeal pipeline covering four independent signal types plus AI-assisted listing analysis.
  3. A measured, honestly-reported cross-domain evaluation of a custom-trained scam classifier that surfaced a real domain-transfer failure before it could reach production scoring.
  4. A documented, partly-benchmarked path to a $0/month public deployment (ONNX-based AI engines, measured image size and RAM footprint) for a resource-constrained student project.

8.3 Limitations (restated and expanded from Chapter 1)

No real CNIC/face accuracy data; the classifier is not production-ready for its actual target domain; the admin panel is incomplete; MFA is unimplemented; device fingerprinting and IP/VPN detection rely on heuristics with acknowledged accuracy limits rather than paid, fully authoritative data sources; no deployment is live; no independent security audit has been performed.
